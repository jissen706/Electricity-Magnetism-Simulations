Electromagnetic Waves

A self-propagating ripple of perpendicular electric and magnetic fields, traveling at c.

Perpendicular E and B, traveling at c

An EM wave is a self-sustaining oscillation: a time-varying E creates a B, and a time-varying B creates an E. They oscillate in phase, point perpendicular to each other, and the whole pattern travels in the direction E × B at speed c.

E(x, t) = E₀ cos(kx − ωt)  ·ŷ     B(x, t) = (E₀/c) cos(kx − ωt)  ·ẑ

c = f λ     ω = 2π f     k = 2π / λ     |E| / |B| = c

Pick any frequency you like — wavelength is forced by λ = c / f. And no matter the amplitude, the magnetic field is always exactly E / c smaller than the electric field (in SI units).

How much energy and momentum the wave carries

The Poynting vector S = (1/μ₀) E × B points along the propagation direction and gives the instantaneous power per unit area carried by the wave. Time-averaging the sinusoid gives the intensity I = E₀²/(2μ₀c) = ½ c ε₀ E₀². When that energy hits a surface, it also delivers momentum, so it produces a pressure: I/c if the surface absorbs everything, 2I/c if it reflects everything.

S(x,t) = (1/μ₀) E·B = c ε₀ E₀² cos²(kx − ωt)    →    I = ⟨S⟩ = E₀² / (2 μ₀ c)

P_abs = I / c    P_refl = 2 I / c    F = P · A    Δp = F · Δt

Reflection delivers twice the momentum kick that absorption does — the photon doesn't just dump its momentum, it reverses, so the surface absorbs both arrivals and departures. (Same reason a ball bouncing elastically off a wall hits twice as hard as one that sticks.)
